Linguistic Phylogenetics

Chapter 18 Synthesis: What Linguistic Phylogenetics Has Found

A quarter of a century after the first Bayesian and likelihood-based language trees, it is fair to ask what the enterprise has actually established. The answer is not a list of results but a list of results together with the conditions under which they hold. Every finding in this chapter rests on particular kinds of data (usually basic-vocabulary cognate sets, sometimes structural features), on particular modelling assumptions (tree-like descent, a clock, a diffusion process, independent evolution of characters), and on evidence that is more or less independent of those assumptions. Some findings survive large changes in data and models and agree with archaeology, genetics and the comparative method; others depend on a single chain of assumptions and have moved with each new analysis. This chapter gathers the substantive findings scattered through the book, states for each the assumptions and evidence on which it rests, and sorts them into what is broadly accepted, what is supported but debated, and what remains contested.

After reading this chapter you should be able to:

  • distinguish broadly accepted, supported-but-debated and contested findings in linguistic phylogenetics, and give one example of each;
  • state, for a given generalization (the Holocene horizon, pulse-pause expansion, frequency-dependent replacement rates), the data, model assumptions and kinds of evidence under which it holds;
  • explain why the recovery of accepted subgroups by a computational tree is not independent validation of the method;
  • evaluate a published root-age or homeland claim by identifying its calibrations, its treatment of ancient languages, its sampling and its diffusion or clock model;
  • classify the evidence offered for a method’s reliability using the validation vocabulary of Section 6.10;
  • explain why comparative claims (about universals, culture or demography) should be evaluated over a posterior sample of trees rather than a single summary tree.

18.1 Taking stock

The modern phase of linguistic phylogenetics is usually dated to a handful of papers published around the turn of the millennium. Gray and Jordan (2000) used maximum parsimony on Austronesian basic vocabulary to test the “express train” model of Austronesian expansion from Taiwan. Ringe et al. (2002) applied compatibility methods to a carefully coded set of lexical, phonological and morphological characters for Indo-European. Gray and Atkinson (2003) used Bayesian inference with rate-smoothed dating to estimate the age of Indo-European. In the years since, the field has grown in three directions at once. Datasets have grown from dozens of languages to hundreds, and, with automated methods, to thousands (Jäger 2018). Models have grown from simple binary substitution processes to stochastic Dollo models, covarion models, relaxed clocks, sampled ancestors and spatial diffusion (Chapters 7 to 11). And the questions asked have widened from “what is the tree?” to “when, where, how fast, and why?”.

It helps to sort the findings into five kinds, which differ greatly in how secure they are.

  1. Classification: which languages group together, and in what order the groups split.
  2. Chronology: when the family and its subgroups began to diverge.
  3. Geography: where the family originated and by which routes it spread.
  4. Process: how language change itself proceeds, including its tempo, its regularities across meanings and features, and its relation to population and society.
  5. Coevolution: how languages and other cultural or biological traits have changed together, using language trees as a scaffold.

The five kinds form a chain of inference, and each link adds assumptions. Classification rests mainly on the cognate data and the substitution model. Chronology adds a clock model, calibrations and a tree prior. Geography adds a model of movement and assumes that present locations carry information about past ones. Process and coevolution findings add models of trait evolution and usually take the tree, its dates and sometimes its locations as given. It is therefore no surprise that classification is generally the most robust kind of finding and coevolution the least: an error at any earlier link propagates forward. In the vocabulary of Section 6.10, the parameters of the later links (an extinction rate, a homeland, a correlation between traits) may be identifiable under the model and yet hard to estimate with realistic samples, sensitive to misspecification, or difficult to interpret as historical quantities.

18.2 Language trees are recoverable, within limits

The most basic finding is that computational methods applied to basic-vocabulary cognate data produce trees that largely agree with the subgroupings established by the comparative method. For Indo-European, every major analysis recovers the well-established subgroups (Germanic, Celtic, Italic, Balto-Slavic, Indo-Iranian, Greek, Armenian, Albanian, Anatolian, Tocharian) and the closer relationships within them (Gray and Atkinson 2003; Bouckaert et al. 2012; Chang et al. 2015; Heggarty et al. 2023). For Austronesian, the Bayesian tree of Gray et al. (2009) matched the main outlines of the subgrouping developed by Austronesianists, including the primary diversity of the Formosan languages of Taiwan and the nested structure of Malayo-Polynesian, Oceanic and Polynesian. The same is true for Bantu (Grollemund et al. 2015), Pama-Nyungan (Bowern and Atkinson 2012) and other families where the phylogenetic results can be compared with expert classifications.

What exactly does this agreement show? It is tempting to read it as independent validation: two methods, applied to the same history, give the same answer. It is not independent, for two reasons. First, the cognate judgements that make up the data matrix are themselves products of the comparative method, made by specialists who usually know the accepted subgrouping, so a tree inferred from those judgements partly reproduces the expertise that went into them. Second, cognate sharing is not the same thing as shared innovation. The comparative method subgroups on innovations; a binary cognate matrix records retentions and innovations alike, and the model must sort them out (Section 15.10). Agreement therefore shows that the phylogenetic summary of the cognate data is consistent with a classification derived, in part, from the same evidence. It does not demonstrate that the method would recover the true history where the comparative method has not already done the work. The Polynesian case study of Chapter 17 makes the point concretely: the tree recovers East Polynesian, the best-established subgroup, but not Tongic, and the disagreements fall where contact and rate variation are suspected (Section 17.14.4).

Stronger evidence of reliability comes from settings where the answer is known independently of the coding. Simulation supplies such settings. Greenhill et al. (2009) simulated borrowing at different rates and with different geographic constraints, and found that tree topologies were robust to the levels of borrowing they considered typical of basic vocabulary, while dates were less robust and tended to be too young, with errors growing as borrowing became more frequent and less geographically restricted. In the vocabulary of Section 6.10, this is a test of robustness under misspecification, and it is only as informative as the borrowing model used to simulate the data: concentrated borrowing between particular neighbours, which is closer to the worst case, can produce confident wrong subgroups even when global accuracy looks high (Section 15.4). The finding is therefore best stated with its conditions: for curated basic-vocabulary lists, in families whose subgroups have been established by the comparative method and from which known loans have been removed, phylogenetic trees reproduce the major subgroups, and simulation suggests that diffuse borrowing at moderate levels does not overturn the topology. The basic vocabulary lists used in phylogenetics were, after all, designed to minimize borrowing.

Where phylogenetic and traditional classifications disagree, the disagreements cluster at the deepest levels of the tree: the order of the first splits within a family, or the position of a branch that separated early and has few shared innovations with any other. These are precisely the parts of the tree where the comparative method is itself uncertain, where short internal branches carry little signal, and where posterior clade probabilities are low. Within Indo-European, for example, the relative order of the early splits after Anatolian and Tocharian, and whether Italic and Celtic form a clade, have remained difficult for both approaches. Phylogenetic methods have not overturned the comparative method’s classifications; they have, at best, quantified uncertainty that philologists already recognized, and occasionally offered support to one of several competing hypotheses.

Trees are, moreover, not the whole story. Measures of treelikeness such as delta scores and Q-residuals vary considerably among families and subgroups (Gray et al. 2010), and some groups, such as dialect continua and the languages of long-standing contact zones, are poorly described by a tree at all (Chapter 8). Gray et al. (2010) argued that the shape of human history is better thought of as a mixture of tree-like and reticulate fabric, with the balance differing between families and periods. Historical glottometry (Kalyan and François 2018) and the broader critique of the tree model by François (2015) make the same point from within historical linguistics: diversification often proceeds through the gradual break-up of dialect networks, in which innovations spread across overlapping areas, rather than through clean splits. A tree inferred from such data is not wrong, but it is a summary that hides the overlapping innovations.

Gray et al. (2009) analyzed basic vocabulary from 400 Austronesian languages with a Bayesian relaxed-clock model and calibrations drawn from archaeology and history. The tree placed the root in Taiwan at about 5,200 years ago, in agreement with the archaeological “out of Taiwan” model and with linguists’ subgrouping. Its most striking finding concerned the tempo of the expansion. Mapping the dated tree onto geography revealed a sequence of pauses, during which the expansion stalled, followed by pulses of rapid dispersal. A pause in Taiwan before the move into the Philippines, and another before the settlement of Polynesia, coincided with the major water gaps that had to be crossed and, the authors argued, with the development of new technologies and social strategies (such as improved sailing technology) that made the next pulse possible. The study showed that a dated language phylogeny can be used to address the dynamics of a prehistoric expansion, not just its starting point. Its conclusions rest on the calibrations (some of them archaeological, so part of the agreement with archaeology is built in), on the relaxed-clock model, and on the assumption that the geography of present-day languages reflects the order of settlement.

Japonic Huisman et al. (2025) about 2,400 (Japanese-Ryukyuan split, 400–500 BCE) consistent with a Yayoi-period spread

Two things stand out. First, the estimates cluster between roughly 2,000 and 10,000 years. This is consistent with the long-held view that the comparative method cannot reach back much further than about eight to ten thousand years, because lexical replacement erodes the signal of common ancestry until it becomes indistinguishable from chance resemblance (Nichols 1992). But the clustering is partly a selection effect and should not be over-interpreted. Phylogenetic dating is applied to families that the comparative method has already recognized, and families older than the comparative method’s ceiling are, by construction, not recognized as families. Every estimate also depends on calibrations (usually historical or archaeological events of the last few thousand years), on a clock model that extrapolates rates from those calibrations to the root, and on the treatment of ancient languages. The defensible statement is that the recognized families that have been dated have Holocene root ages under the calibrations and clock models used, not that language families in general are no older than the Holocene. Phylogenetic methods have not convincingly broken the ceiling. The most prominent attempt, the claim by Pagel et al. (2013) that a set of “ultraconserved” words supports a Eurasiatic superfamily about 15,000 years old, was strongly criticized for relying on reconstructed proto-forms of uneven quality and on similarity judgements that could reflect chance (Heggarty 2013). Global trees inferred from automated cognate judgements (Jäger 2018) are useful for comparative studies, but their deepest nodes are not interpreted as evidence for macrofamilies.

Second, for the few families with several independent estimates, the estimates differ by more than their nominal credible intervals would suggest. The two Sino-Tibetan studies, using different data and calibrations, differ by more than a millennium, although they agree on a northern Chinese origin. For Indo-European, the estimates range from about 6,000 to almost 10,000 years. The differences arise not from errors in the MCMC but from different data (which meanings, which cognate judgements, which languages), different models (in particular the treatment of ancient languages as tips or as ancestors) and different calibrations (Chapters 10 and 15). A credible interval is conditional on the data and the model; it does not capture uncertainty about the choice of data and model. Robust chronological conclusions require multiple analyses with different, independently justified choices, and a root age should be reported as an estimate under stated assumptions rather than as a discovery. Recent simulation work sharpens the point. The Bayesian samplers used for tip-dating appear to be well calibrated for their own models (King 2026a), but on Indo-European histories simulated under a richer model, with loans and rate variation, published dating methods did not reliably recover the simulated root age and their intervals understated the error (Rönchen, Billing, et al. 2025).

The Indo-European controversy is the most thoroughly studied case of competing dates, and it illustrates how much rests on modelling choices; Section 15.9 treats it in detail. The Anatolian hypothesis (Renfrew 1987) links the spread of Indo-European to the spread of farming from Anatolia beginning about 9,000 years ago; the steppe hypothesis (Anthony 2007) links it to the expansion of pastoralists from the Pontic-Caspian steppe about 6,000 to 5,000 years ago. The analyses of Gray and Atkinson (2003) and Bouckaert et al. (2012) estimated the older date, and the second also inferred an Anatolian homeland using a phylogeographic model. Chang et al. (2015) argued that ancient languages such as Latin, Old Irish and Vedic Sanskrit are very close to the ancestors of their modern descendants, and that treating them as distant cousins inflates the estimated rate of change and therefore the root age. Constraining these ancient languages to be ancestral, they obtained a root age of about 6,000 years (medians of roughly 5,900 to 6,500 years across their main analyses), compatible with the steppe hypothesis. Meanwhile, ancient DNA revealed a massive migration from the steppe into Europe around 5,000 years ago (Haak et al. 2015), which many took as decisive for the steppe hypothesis, at least for the European branches. Heggarty et al. (2023) then assembled a new, carefully curated cognate database (IE-CoR) with explicit protocols for meaning and cognate coding, modelled ancient languages as sampled ancestors, and estimated a root age of about 8,100 years. They proposed a hybrid scenario, in which the family originated south of the Caucasus and some branches later spread through the steppe, compatible with ancient DNA evidence for population movements from the south (Lazaridis et al. 2022). The debate is not resolved: the IE-CoR data are now openly available for reanalysis (Anderson et al. 2025), critics from the lexicostatistical tradition have questioned parts of its cognate coding and the adequacy of a 170-meaning list (Kassian and Starostin 2025), and critics of the whole phylogenetic approach have questioned its foundations (Pereltsvaig and Lewis 2015). What the episode demonstrates is that the root age is not robust to defensible changes in the treatment of ancient languages and in data design. It has also forced every side to make its assumptions explicit, and the convergence of linguistic, archaeological and genetic evidence on more nuanced scenarios is itself a product of the controversy.

18.4 Homelands and expansions: farming, and not only farming

The farming/language dispersal hypothesis holds that many of the world’s large language families spread with the expansion of early agricultural populations, whose demographic growth allowed them to spread into areas occupied by hunter-gatherers (Bellwood and Renfrew 2002; Diamond and Bellwood 2003; Bellwood 2005). Linguistic phylogenetics offers some of the most direct tests of this idea, because dated trees can be compared with the archaeological chronology of farming. The test is only as independent as the calibrations: where a tree is calibrated with archaeological dates that are themselves tied to the farming hypothesis, agreement with that hypothesis is partly circular (Section 15.7).

The results are mixed in an informative way. Several families have root ages and homelands consistent with agricultural expansions. Austronesian originated in Taiwan at about the time that rice and millet farming appear in the archaeological record of the region (Gray et al. 2009). The Sino-Tibetan studies both locate the origin among millet farmers of northern China (Sagart et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2019). The Bantu expansion, whose phylogeny and routes were reconstructed by Currie et al. (2013), Grollemund et al. (2015) and Koile et al. (2022), is closely associated with the spread of farming (and later iron working) across sub-Saharan Africa. Japonic is dated to about the time of the arrival of wet-rice agriculture in the Japanese archipelago (Lee and Hasegawa 2011). Robbeets et al. (2021) argued that a Transeurasian family spread with millet farming from northeast China, combining linguistic, archaeological and genetic evidence in what they called “triangulation”. The Anatolian version of the Indo-European homeland is itself a farming dispersal hypothesis.

But the farming model is not universal, and some of the most compelling phylogeographic results concern expansions that had nothing to do with agriculture. Pama-Nyungan, which covers most of Australia, spread among hunter-gatherers; Bouckaert et al. (2018) inferred a mid-Holocene origin in the Gulf Plains region of northern Australia and an expansion that was not driven by farming. Semitic originated in the Levant in the Early Bronze Age, millennia after the origins of farming in the region (Kitchen et al. 2009). The steppe version of Indo-European origins links it to mobile pastoralism. And the Transeurasian proposal remains contested at a more basic level, since many specialists doubt that the Turkic, Mongolic, Tungusic, Koreanic and Japonic families are related at all. A fair summary is that the dated trees are consistent with agriculture having been one important driver of language expansions, but that other demographic and social processes, including pastoralism, technological innovation, and changes in social organization, have also produced large language families.

The phylogeographic methods behind these homeland inferences (Chapter 11) deserve their own caveats. They assume that the present locations of languages reflect their past movements reasonably well, so that a family’s homeland lies near the centroid of its deepest lineages. Language replacement, long-distance migration and the extinction of entire branches (as happened to many early Indo-European languages of the steppe and Anatolia) can mislead them. Sampling matters too: a homeland inference can be pulled toward the region with the densest sampling of languages. A posterior distribution over the root location, moreover, is a statement under the diffusion model; it does not show that the model describes how speech communities actually moved. In the vocabulary of Section 6.10, the root location may be identifiable under the model without being straightforwardly interpretable as a historical homeland. Homeland estimates should be computed over the posterior sample of dated trees rather than on a single summary tree, since uncertainty in topology and dates propagates into the location. These problems do not invalidate phylogeographic inference, but they mean that homeland estimates are only as good as the coverage of the family, ancient and modern, and they gain greatly in credibility when supported by independent archaeological or genetic evidence.

18.5 Pulses, pauses and routes

Dated phylogenies have been used to infer not just when expansions began but how they proceeded. The pulse-pause pattern found for Austronesian (Gray et al. 2009) has its counterparts elsewhere. In the Bantu expansion, Grollemund et al. (2015) found that the spread of Bantu-speaking populations was shaped by habitat: populations tended to track their preferred savanna environments, moving more slowly through the rainforest, and the opening of savanna corridors during a dry period appears to have facilitated the expansion southward. The route through or around the central African rainforest has long been debated; Koile et al. (2022) used a phylogeographic analysis to support a route through the rainforest rather than around it. For Arawakan in lowland South America, Walker and Ribeiro (2011) inferred an origin in western Amazonia and an expansion along river systems. For the Tupi family, Walker et al. (2012) used a distance-based phylogeny and geography to examine its spread across lowland South America.

These studies share a common logic. A dated tree with locations at the tips allows the reconstruction of ancestral locations along every branch, and thus of the speed and direction of movement through time. Environmental features such as coastlines, rivers, mountains and vegetation zones can then be tested as influences on the movement. This is one of the areas where linguistic phylogenetics has borrowed most directly from biology, in particular from viral phylogeography, and where the borrowing has been most productive. The inferred tempo and routes are, however, conditional on three things: the resolution of the dates (a “pause” is only detectable if it is longer than the uncertainty in the node ages that bound it), the diffusion model (random diffusion on a sphere cannot easily capture movement along a coast or a river, and landscape-aware models remain underused; Chapter 11), and the sampling of languages across the region. The debate over the Bantu route shows that different diffusion models and datasets can support different routes. Pulse-pause and route findings are therefore best described as supported for the best-studied cases, Austronesian and Bantu, and still debated in their details.

In biology, comparative phylogeography asks whether several co-distributed lineages responded to the same historical event at the same time, for example whether many species expanded simultaneously after the last glacial maximum. Hierarchical approximate Bayesian computation was developed to test for such synchronous divergence or expansion across taxa (Hickerson et al. 2006). Linguistic phylogenetics has so far studied families one at a time. A comparative phylogeographic analysis could ask, for instance, whether the expansions of several families in the same region (in mainland Southeast Asia, in Amazonia, or in the Sahel) were synchronous and thus plausibly driven by a common climatic or technological cause. To our knowledge this has rarely if ever been attempted with language data, although the dated trees needed for it are increasingly available.

18.6 The tempo of lexical change

Some of the most general and theoretically interesting findings of linguistic phylogenetics concern the process of language change itself. Because they are estimated as parameters of models fitted on trees, they inherit the assumptions of both the tree and the model of change.

18.6.1 Rates vary predictably among meanings

The best-supported finding about lexical change is that the rate at which words are replaced varies enormously among meanings, and that this variation is not random. Pagel et al. (2007) estimated replacement rates for the 200 meanings of the Swadesh list across Indo-European and found that rates varied roughly a hundredfold. The words for numerals, pronouns and some other very frequent meanings were replaced extremely slowly, while words for meanings such as ‘dirty’, ‘to turn’ or ‘to throw’ changed rapidly. Frequency of use in everyday speech was a strong predictor of the rate, with frequently used words changing more slowly, and the pattern was similar across the Indo-European languages examined. Frequencies of use for the same meanings are themselves strikingly similar across unrelated languages (Calude and Pagel 2011), which suggests that the relationship reflects general properties of language use rather than a peculiarity of Indo-European. Further work has found that semantic properties, such as how concrete a meaning is and how many related meanings it is connected to, also predict replacement rates (Vejdemo and Hörberg 2016).

The conditions are worth separating. That rates vary among meanings is supported in every family studied and by the large improvement in fit that rate-heterogeneity models provide (Chapter 7). The specific relationship with frequency rests most heavily on Indo-European, on frequency counts from a few modern corpora projected back in time, and on a fixed meaning list in which meaning slots are treated as independent; its extension to other families is suggested rather than demonstrated. Rate estimates for individual meanings are also affected by how synonyms and borrowings are coded (Section 15.3). Practically, the finding explains why glottochronology’s assumption of a single, universal rate of replacement failed (Chapter 9), and why modern models must include rate variation among meanings. Theoretically, it is consistent with the view that language change is shaped by the cognitive and social processes of language use, and it parallels the finding in molecular evolution that functionally important genes evolve more slowly.

18.6.2 Change is partly punctuational

Biologists have long debated whether evolutionary change accumulates gradually or is concentrated at speciation events. The same question can be asked of languages, and dated trees make it testable.

Atkinson et al. (2008) tested whether languages accumulate lexical change gradually or whether change is concentrated at the moments when languages split. If splitting itself causes change, for example through founder effects or through speakers emphasizing differences to mark a new identity, then languages on lineages with more splitting events should have accumulated more change, a pattern known in biology as the node-density effect. Analyzing Bantu, Indo-European and Austronesian, they found evidence of such punctuational bursts, which they estimated to account for roughly 10 to 33 percent of the total lexical divergence. The finding suggests that some lexical change is tied to the social processes of language splitting rather than to the passage of time alone, and it has implications for dating, since a clock model that ignores punctuational change will misattribute some change to time (Chapter 9). The inference rests on counting nodes along root-to-tip paths, and node counts depend on how densely each part of the tree is sampled and on errors in tree reconstruction, which can themselves produce an apparent node-density effect. The result is therefore supported, but the artefact has to be ruled out anew in each dataset.

18.6.3 Grammar is not more stable than vocabulary

A long tradition in historical linguistics holds that grammatical structure is more stable than vocabulary, and therefore a better guide to deep relationships. Phylogenetic analyses have tested this claim directly and mostly failed to support it. Greenhill, Atkinson, et al. (2010) compared the phylogenetic signal and tempo of structural and lexical data in Austronesian, and Greenhill et al. (2017) carried out a larger and more detailed comparison of rates of change in grammatical features and basic vocabulary across Austronesian languages. They found that grammatical features, taken as a class, were not more stable than basic vocabulary; many structural features changed faster than the average basic vocabulary item, and the lineages with unusually fast change in one system were often not the ones with fast change in the other. The two systems appear to respond to different evolutionary pressures. The comparison is conditional on how features are coded (binary or multistate, and which features are included) and on a tree inferred from the lexicon, and its strongest evidence comes from one family. Studies of the stability of individual typological features, using a variety of methods, have found that some features are much more stable than others (Wichmann and Holman 2009b; Dediu 2011; Dediu and Cysouw 2013). Agreement among methods about which features are stable is only partial, however, which is itself an important caution against using any single “stable” feature as evidence of deep relationship. The finding that some structural features retain phylogenetic signal at considerable time depths has nonetheless encouraged their use in difficult cases, such as the structural phylogenetics of the Papuan languages of island Melanesia (Dunn et al. 2005).

18.7 Universals: lineage-specific or universal?

Typologists have long sought universal dependencies between grammatical features, such as Greenberg’s correlation between verb-object order and the order of adposition and noun. Phylogenetic comparative methods (Chapter 13) offer a way to test such dependencies while accounting for the non-independence of related languages. The most influential test, by Dunn et al. (2011), used Bayesian models of correlated evolution on trees of four families (Austronesian, Bantu, Indo-European and Uto-Aztecan) and found that the dependencies between word-order features differed from family to family. They concluded that word-order correlations are largely lineage-specific, the product of cultural evolution within families rather than of universal cognitive constraints. The paper prompted vigorous responses. Critics pointed out that four families provide a small and possibly unrepresentative sample, that the power to detect dependencies within single families is limited, and that the results were not as inconsistent with Greenberg’s correlations as the paper suggested (Dryer 2011).

Later work has qualified the original conclusion. Using a much larger sample of languages and families, phylogenies for every lineage and explicit model comparison, Jäger and Wahle (2021) found credible correlations for many of the classic word-order pairs, in line with Dryer’s “verb patterners”. They also found that a model with the same dynamics in every lineage fitted better than a lineage-specific one, so their data gave no clear support for lineage-specific dependencies. Reconstruction of ancestral word orders has also suggested systematic tendencies in the direction of change (Maurits and Griffiths 2014). The emerging picture is that both views capture part of the truth. There are cross-linguistic tendencies in how features co-evolve, but they are tendencies rather than laws, they can be overridden by lineage-specific histories, and detecting them requires large, phylogenetically diverse samples. The largest test so far, of 191 proposed universals on Grambank with models that account for both descent and geography, found support for about a third of them, mostly word-order and hierarchical universals, and evolutionary analyses suggesting that languages tend to change toward the preferred patterns (Verkerk, Shcherbakova, et al. 2025). Two assumptions run through all of these analyses: that the trees (for most families, classifications with arbitrary branch lengths) are adequate scaffolds, and that contact between languages can be neglected or modelled separately. Such results should be checked over posterior samples of trees, or at least over alternative branch-length schemes, because a single summary tree can distort inferred rates and dependencies (Section 17.12). The release of Grambank (Skirgård et al. 2023) has made large-scale tests much easier. Analyses of Grambank data have also shown that shared descent accounts for a substantial part of the variation in grammatical structure, more than spatial proximity, which reinforces the case for taking phylogeny into account in any typological analysis.

18.8 Population, society and environment

A set of high-profile studies has asked whether the properties and rates of change of languages depend on the populations that speak them or on their environments. Results here are less secure than for the themes above, and several have been contested. They sit at the far end of the chain of inference described in Section 18.1: they depend on trees, on measures of population and environment that are usually modern proxies for past conditions, and on controls for contact and areal clustering.

Population size and rates of change. Theory from population genetics suggests that the rate of change in a population can depend on its size, though in different directions depending on whether change is driven by drift or by innovation. Bromham et al. (2015) analyzed Polynesian languages and found that larger populations gained new words faster, while smaller populations lost existing words faster. Greenhill et al. (2018) extended the analysis to Indo-European, Austronesian and Bantu, and did not find a consistent relationship between population size and rates of lexical change across the three families. The effect of population size on rates is therefore not established as a general law.

Population size and complexity. Lupyan and Dale (2010) found, in a large non-phylogenetic sample from WALS, that languages with more speakers tend to have simpler inflectional morphology, and proposed that this reflects the influence of adult second-language learners. The claim has been widely discussed. Using Grambank data and phylogenetically informed models, Shcherbakova et al. (2023) found no evidence that languages spoken in larger societies, or by more non-native speakers, have less grammatical complexity. This is a clear example of how adding phylogenetic control can overturn an influential correlational finding.

Environment. Everett et al. (2015) reported that languages with complex tone systems are rare in very dry climates, and proposed a physiological mechanism involving the effect of dry air on the vocal folds. The finding was challenged on several grounds, including the clustering of tonal languages in a few families and areas and the difficulty of separating environmental effects from shared history and contact (Hammarström 2016). More generally, Roberts and Winters (2013) showed that cross-linguistic data, analyzed without proper control for history and geography, readily produce strong but spurious correlations with arbitrary cultural variables. The lesson for the field is methodological: claims about environmental or demographic causes of linguistic features require phylogenetic and spatial controls, robustness checks across families and across trees, and, ideally, a mechanism that can be tested independently.

Endangerment. Phylogenetic and comparative methods are also being used to understand the threats to linguistic diversity. Bromham et al. (2022) modelled the predictors of language endangerment worldwide, finding that factors such as the density of roads and the level of schooling in a region are associated with higher endangerment, and predicting a substantial loss of languages within the coming century unless action is taken. These are associations in observational data and projections under the fitted model, not demonstrated causes. Skirgård et al. (2023) emphasized that the loss of endangered languages would remove much of the structural diversity currently documented in some regions.

18.9 Language trees as scaffolds for cultural evolution

Language trees have become the standard scaffold for studying the evolution of culture in human societies, because languages are usually transmitted along the same lines as many other cultural traits (Chapter 14). The program was launched by Mace and Pagel (1994), who argued that the comparative method of evolutionary biology could solve Galton’s problem in anthropology. Since then, studies using language phylogenies have reconstructed the ancestral states and evolutionary dynamics of a wide range of traits. Holden and Mace (2003) showed that the adoption of cattle in Bantu-speaking societies was associated with the loss of matrilineal descent. Jordan et al. (2009) inferred that early Austronesian societies were probably matrilocal, and Fortunato and Jordan (2010) compared the evolution of marital residence in Indo-European and Austronesian. Currie, Greenhill, Gray, et al. (2010) found that political complexity in Austronesian societies rose, and in the best-fitting model also fell, in small sequential steps, with a model that allowed larger drops fitting almost as well. Watts et al. (2016) reported that ritual human sacrifice was associated with the emergence and maintenance of social stratification in Austronesian societies, and Watts, Greenhill, et al. (2015) found that broad supernatural punishment, but not moralizing high gods, tended to precede political complexity in the same societies. Silva and Tehrani (2016) used Indo-European trees to argue that some folktales, such as “The Smith and the Devil”, may go back to Proto-Indo-European times.

The collective message of this work, summarized by Gray and Watts (2017), is that cultural evolution has macroevolutionary regularities that can be studied with the same quantitative tools as biological macroevolution: some cultural traits are strongly conserved along lineages, others change rapidly; some changes are directional, others reversible; and traits co-evolve in ways that can be tested. Each such result is conditional on three assumptions: that the cultural trait was transmitted along the language lineages rather than horizontally between neighbours, that the trait was coded consistently across societies described by different ethnographers, and that the language tree, with its dates, is a reasonable scaffold. The better studies carry tree uncertainty through by repeating the analysis over a posterior sample of trees; analyses on a single summary tree can overstate the confidence of an ancestral state or of the order of two changes, even when the summary topology looks plausible (Section 17.12). With these qualifications, the program has turned speculative narratives about cultural history into testable hypotheses.

18.10 What the methods themselves have taught us

Beyond its substantive findings, linguistic phylogenetics has learned several lessons about its own methods, which recur throughout this book.

Data matter more than models. The largest differences between published analyses of the same family stem from the data: which languages are included, which meanings are used, how cognates are judged and coded, and how ancient languages are treated. The re-analysis of Indo-European with the IE-CoR database (Heggarty et al. 2023) was motivated as much by data design as by modelling. Investments in carefully curated, openly available data, of the kind represented by Lexibank, Grambank and IE-CoR (Appendix G), have arguably done more for the reliability of results than any new model.

Priors and calibrations shape dates. Dates depend strongly on calibrations, tree priors and clock models, and on how these interact (Chapters 9 and 10). Sensitivity analyses and prior-only runs should be routine, and the uncertainty arising from defensible alternative choices should be reported alongside the credible intervals of a single preferred analysis.

Model adequacy is rarely checked. Most published analyses choose among a handful of models by marginal likelihood or information criteria, but few ask whether even the best model can reproduce key features of the data, such as the distribution of cognate-class sizes or the number of singletons per language. Posterior predictive checks of empirical model adequacy are well developed in biology and readily applicable (Sections 15.12 and 17.15). The first systematic check of the standard set-up found that the covarion model fails to reproduce several features of Indo-European cognate data, and that a default setting for partition rates miscalibrated the posterior (King 2026b).

Better fit is not always better history. A related lesson, still under debate, is that the model with the best marginal likelihood need not recover the history best. Rönchen (2025) argues that finite-state models such as the covarion model, combined with the ascertainment correction used for cognate data, overfit, so that they can fit better than Dollo-type models and still infer less accurate trees (Rönchen, Häuser, et al. 2025). Whether or not this result holds generally, it reinforces the case for judging models by simulation from realistic generating processes as well as by relative fit.

Different kinds of validation answer different questions. The trust the field places in its methods rests on several kinds of evidence that are easily conflated (Section 6.10). Most simulation studies (Barbançon et al. 2013; Greenhill et al. 2009) test parameter recovery (data simulated under the model’s own assumptions) or robustness under misspecification (data simulated with borrowing or rate variation that the model ignores), and they are only as realistic as the simulator. Agreement with families of “known” history is weaker evidence than it appears, because the known history and the cognate coding both come from the comparative method; the cleanest out-of-sample tests use historically attested dates or splits that were not used as calibrations. Empirical model adequacy on real data, and the historical interpretation of estimated parameters, are the least developed. What these studies have taught is where methods fail (short internal branches, concentrated contact, misplaced ancient languages) as much as where they succeed.

Openness and reproducibility pay off. The adoption of shared data standards (CLDF) and tools that generate analysis files from short configuration files (BEASTling) has made analyses easier to reproduce and extend, and has made it possible to compare results across families. Chapter 17 gives a practical template, and its software runs show that even published tools need maintenance if analyses are to remain reproducible.

18.12 Open questions and the next decade

What will the next decade bring? The following questions seem likely to drive research, and each connects to methods from biology that are only beginning to be used in linguistics (Chapter 16, which grades each method by how far its transfer to language data has been tested).

How deep can we go? The time ceiling of the comparative method is a property of lexical data, not of the methods themselves. Progress beyond it, if it is possible at all, will probably require different kinds of data: structural features with demonstrably slow rates of change, sound-level models that can distinguish regular correspondences from chance resemblance, or combinations of data types in total-evidence analyses. Models of sequence-level sound change are emerging. Bouchard-Côté et al. (2013) reconstructed ancestral word forms automatically with probabilistic models of sound change, Hruschka et al. (2015) modelled regular sound changes as events of concerted evolution, and Goldstein et al. (2026) inferred trees directly from aligned cognate word forms with an insertion-deletion model borrowed from molecular evolution. Scaling such models up to phylogenetic inference is a major open challenge.

How can borrowing be modelled explicitly? Most analyses still treat borrowing as noise, either by excluding known loans or by hoping that its effect is small. Models that include lateral transfer explicitly, such as the stochastic Dollo model with lateral transfer of Kelly and Nicholls (2017), and Bayesian models that detect contact events on a tree (Neureiter et al. 2022), are beginning to change this. A mature theory would infer the tree and the contact events jointly, estimate their timing, and test hypotheses about which kinds of vocabulary are borrowed under which social conditions. Biological methods for reticulate evolution, from gene-tree/species-tree reconciliation to phylogenetic networks, offer a rich toolbox (Chapters 8 and 16).

Can linguistic, genetic and archaeological histories be integrated formally? “Triangulation” across disciplines is currently mostly qualitative: separate analyses are compared after the fact (Robbeets et al. 2021). Joint models that relate language trees to genetic population histories, and that estimate where and when they diverge, would allow formal tests of whether languages spread by migration or by language shift. Large databases that link genetic and linguistic data for the same populations (Barbieri et al. 2022) make this increasingly feasible, and the mismatches between genetic and linguistic histories are as informative as the matches.

What drives diversification? Why do some families have thousands of languages while others have a handful? Biological diversification models that relate speciation and extinction rates to traits and environments (Chapter 16) have barely been applied to languages. Applying them requires care, since language “extinction” and “speciation” are cultural processes with their own dynamics, since sampling of extinct languages is usually very incomplete, and since many diversification parameters are not identifiable from trees of living lineages alone. But they could turn longstanding questions about the drivers of linguistic diversity into quantitative tests.

How can phylogenies inform the preservation of diversity? Conservation biology uses phylogenies to prioritize the protection of evolutionarily distinct species. The same logic could help identify languages whose loss would remove the most unique linguistic history.

Conservation biologists measure the evolutionary history represented by a set of species with phylogenetic diversity, the total length of the branches of the tree that connect them (Faith 1992). The EDGE approach (“evolutionarily distinct and globally endangered”) combines a species’ evolutionary distinctiveness, measured from its share of the tree, with its extinction risk to set conservation priorities (Isaac et al. 2007). The analogy with endangered languages is direct. A language isolate, or the last speaker community of an ancient branch of a family, represents far more unique history than one of many closely related dialects, and global trees and endangerment data are now available (Jäger 2018; Bromham et al. 2022). Phylogenetic diversity measures and EDGE-like scores could thus inform the allocation of scarce documentation resources. To our knowledge such measures have rarely been applied systematically to languages, and their use would raise interesting questions about how to treat isolates and about which branch lengths (lexical, structural, or temporal) best capture “linguistic history”.

Will automation transform the field? Automated cognate detection, phonetic alignment and data standardization have already made it possible to analyze thousands of languages (Jäger 2018; List et al. 2017). As these methods improve, the bottleneck will shift from data assembly to data quality and to the interpretation of results. The central challenge will be to combine the scale of automated methods with the precision of expert philological work, rather than to replace one with the other. Recent results point both ways. Characters derived from automatic alignments of word forms can rival expert cognates within families and outperform them across families, at least for topology (Jäger 2025); but the cognate datasets available are small, which limits parameter-rich models and machine learning (Häuser et al. 2026), and trees can change with seemingly innocuous upstream choices such as how concepts are translated into words (Snee et al. 2026).

How should results be evaluated? As analyses become larger and more complex, the field needs agreed standards for reporting, for validating models, and for archiving data and posterior samples. The most important development of the next decade may not be a new model but the routine use of simulation-based validation, posterior predictive checks and transparent sensitivity analyses, so that the reliability of a published phylogeny can be judged by its readers rather than taken on trust.

Linguistic phylogenetics began as an attempt to answer old questions about the history of language families with new tools. It has answered some of them, provisionally and under stated assumptions, and has shown that others are harder than they looked. Its most lasting contribution may turn out to be the habit of stating historical hypotheses as explicit probabilistic models, which can be tested, compared, and improved.

Findings in linguistic phylogenetics form a chain (classification, chronology, geography, process, coevolution); each link adds assumptions, so security generally decreases along the chain. Basic-vocabulary trees reproduce the major subgroups established by the comparative method, but this agreement is not independent validation: the cognate judgements come from the comparative method, and cognate sharing is not shared innovation. Disagreements concentrate on short, deep branches where both approaches are uncertain. The recognized families that have been dated have Holocene root ages under the calibrations and clock models used, a pattern that partly reflects which families can be recognized at all; estimates for the same family can differ by more than their credible intervals because of differences in data, models and the treatment of ancient languages. Dated trees are consistent with many families having spread with farmers, but others, notably Pama-Nyungan, spread among hunter-gatherers or pastoralists, and inferred homelands and routes are conditional on diffusion models, sampling and the resolution of dates. Rates of lexical replacement vary about a hundredfold among meanings; the link with frequency rests mainly on Indo-European; punctuational change and the relative instability of grammar are supported but debated. Claims about universals, population size and environment have often weakened once phylogeny is properly controlled, and comparative and cultural analyses should be run over posterior samples of trees. Simulation studies are mostly tests of parameter recovery or robustness; empirical model adequacy and historical interpretation remain the least developed forms of validation.

18.14 Further reading

  • Gray et al. (2010): A short, influential essay on the balance between tree-like and reticulate history in human populations, with a framework for measuring treelikeness that remains useful.
  • Bowern (2018): A concise review in the Annual Review of Linguistics of computational phylogenetics in linguistics, covering data, methods, results and controversies.
  • Greenhill et al. (2020): An accessible handbook chapter on Bayesian phylolinguistics by three of its leading practitioners, explaining the main methods and results.
  • Gray and Watts (2017): A review of the cultural macroevolution program, explaining why language trees matter for understanding the evolution of human societies and summarizing results from Austronesian and other families.
  • Heggarty et al. (2023) and Chang et al. (2015). Read together, the two most methodologically careful analyses of Indo-European origins show how data design and model choice shape the results.
  • Pagel et al. (2007) and Greenhill et al. (2017). Two landmark studies of the tempo of language change, one on lexical rates and frequency, the other on grammar and vocabulary.
  • Dunn et al. (2011) and Jäger and Wahle (2021). The debate over lineage-specific versus universal word-order dependencies, from the original provocation to the large-scale reassessment.
  • Bellwood (2005): The fullest statement of the farming/language dispersal hypothesis, essential background for the dates and homelands discussed in this chapter.
  • Nichols (1992): A classic study of linguistic diversity in space and time, whose discussion of the time limits of the comparative method frames the question of how deep phylogenetic methods can reach.
  • List (2024): Ten open problems of computational historical linguistics, revisited in the light of recent progress; a useful agenda to set against this chapter’s list.
  • Rönchen (2025) and King (2026b): Two recent evaluations of the standard models of vocabulary evolution, by simulation and by posterior predictive checks, whose conclusions any future analysis will need to address.
