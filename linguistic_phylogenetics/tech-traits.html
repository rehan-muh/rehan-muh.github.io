D.1 Chapter 11: Phylogeography and Homelands

D.1.1 Pruning for discrete locations

In discrete phylogeography (Section 11.4.1) the location of a lineage is one of \(K\) regions, evolving under a rate matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) with transition probabilities \(\mathbf{P}(t) = e^{\mathbf{Q}t}\). The likelihood of the observed tip regions is computed by Felsenstein’s pruning algorithm. For each node \(v\) and each region \(i\), the partial likelihood \(L_v(i)\) is the probability of the tip regions below \(v\) given that \(v\) was in region \(i\). It is computed from the tips toward the root: \[\begin{equation} L_v(i) = \prod_{c \in \text{children}(v)} \sum_{j=1}^{K} P_{ij}(t_c)\, L_c(j), \tag{D.1} \end{equation}\] with \(L_v(i) = 1\) if tip \(v\) is in region \(i\) and \(0\) otherwise, and \(t_c\) the length of the branch leading to child \(c\). The likelihood of the whole tree is \(\sum_i \pi_i L_{\text{root}}(i)\), where \(\pi_i\) is a prior on the root region, and the posterior probability that the root was in region \(i\) is \(\pi_i L_{\text{root}}(i) / \sum_j \pi_j L_{\text{root}}(j)\). Marginal probabilities at other internal nodes need a second, root-to-tips pass, or equivalently re-rooting at each node in turn (Section 12.4.2). An implementation of the recursion for any discrete character is given in Section D.2.2.

D.1.2 Route Bayes factors in BSSVS

Under the BSSVS prior of Equation (11.3), the number of active symmetric routes is \(K - 1\) plus a Poisson variable with mean \(\ln 2\), so the expected number of active routes is \(\ln 2 + K - 1\) out of \(K(K-1)/2\) possible ones. The prior inclusion probability of any one route is therefore \(q = (\ln 2 + K - 1) / [K(K-1)/2]\). If \(p_{ij}\) is the posterior inclusion probability of route \(i \leftrightarrow j\) (the posterior mean of \(\delta_{ij}\), read from the MCMC log), the Bayes factor for including the route is the ratio of posterior to prior odds: \[\begin{equation} \text{BF}_{ij} = \frac{p_{ij}/(1 - p_{ij})}{q/(1 - q)}, \qquad q = \frac{\ln 2 + K - 1}{K(K-1)/2}. \tag{D.2} \end{equation}\] The function below computes it. The posterior inclusion probabilities are invented, as if read from a BEAST log file for an analysis with four regions.

bssvs_bf <- function(post_incl, K, poisson_mean = log(2)) {
  q <- (poisson_mean + K - 1) / (K * (K - 1) / 2)  # prior inclusion probability
  post_incl <- pmin(post_incl, 1 - 1e-6)         # guard against p = 1
  (post_incl / (1 - post_incl)) / (q / (1 - q))
}
post <- c("R1-R2" = 0.99, "R1-R3" = 0.12, "R1-R4" = 0.55,
          "R2-R3" = 0.81, "R2-R4" = 0.08, "R3-R4" = 0.93)
round(bssvs_bf(post, K = 4), 1)
#> R1-R2 R1-R3 R1-R4 R2-R3 R2-R4 R3-R4 
#>  61.8   0.1   0.8   2.7   0.1   8.3
# the same posterior inclusion probability with 4 and with 20 regions
round(c(K4 = bssvs_bf(0.81, K = 4), K20 = bssvs_bf(0.81, K = 20)), 1)
#>   K4  K20 
#>  2.7 36.9

With four regions the prior inclusion probability is about 0.62, so a posterior inclusion probability of 0.81 gives a Bayes factor below 3; with twenty regions the prior inclusion probability is about 0.1 and the same posterior gives a Bayes factor above 30. A posterior inclusion probability that hits 1 in every sample gives an infinite Bayes factor; the guard in the function caps it, and in practice such routes are reported as “BF > 1000” or similar. Because the Poisson prior can be changed by the analyst, and because \(q\) depends on \(K\), route Bayes factors should always be reported together with \(K\) and the prior.

D.1.3 The multivariate normal model for coordinates

Under bivariate Brownian motion (Equation (11.5)), the location of each tip is the root location plus the sum of independent normal displacements along the branches from the root to the tip. Two tips \(i\) and \(j\) share the displacements on the path from the root to their most recent common ancestor, so the covariance between any coordinate of \(i\) and any coordinate of \(j\) is the corresponding entry of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) multiplied by the length of the shared path, \(C_{ij}\). Writing \(\mathbf{C}\) for the \(n \times n\) phylogenetic covariance matrix (with \(C_{ii}\) the root-to-tip time of tip \(i\)) and stacking the coordinates of the \(n\) tips into a vector \(\mathbf{X}\) of length \(2n\) (tip by tip), \[\begin{equation} \mathbf{X} \sim \mathcal{N}_{2n}\!\left(\mathbf{1}_n \otimes \mathbf{x}_{\text{root}},\; \mathbf{C} \otimes \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right), \tag{D.3} \end{equation}\] where \(\otimes\) is the Kronecker product. The likelihood can be evaluated directly from this formula for small trees, or in time linear in the number of tips by a pruning algorithm analogous to Equation (D.1), in which the partial likelihoods are normal densities summarized by their means and variances (Felsenstein 1985b).

For a single coordinate \(\mathbf{x}\) the model is \(\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}_n(x_{\text{root}} \mathbf{1}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{C})\), a linear model with a single intercept and correlated errors. Maximizing the log-likelihood \[\ell(x_{\text{root}}, \sigma^2) = -\tfrac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \tfrac12 \log\det\mathbf{C} - \frac{(\mathbf{x} - x_{\text{root}}\mathbf{1})^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - x_{\text{root}}\mathbf{1})}{2\sigma^2}\] with respect to \(x_{\text{root}}\) gives the generalized least squares estimator of Equation (11.6) (Section A.11). Its variance follows from the general GLS result \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) with the design matrix \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}\). Note that neither the weights \(\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1} / \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1} \mathbf{1}\) nor the variance depends on the observed locations.

The chunk below recreates the tree and locations of Section 11.13.1 and checks that the GLS mean equals the maximum-likelihood root location returned by fastAnc().

D.1.4 Why standardized contrasts serve as an adequacy statistic

The adequacy check of Section 11.13.3 uses the standardized independent contrasts of a coordinate (Section D.3.4). Under Brownian motion on the correct tree, the \(n - 1\) standardized contrasts are independent and identically distributed \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), whatever the shape of the tree. Their kurtosis, \(\overline{u^4} / (\overline{u^2})^2\), is invariant to \(\sigma^2\), so its null distribution can be simulated from standard normal draws without reference to the tree. A relaxed random walk inflates the variance of the contrasts on some branches and not others, which produces a mixture of normals with heavier tails than any single normal, and so a larger kurtosis.

A directional trend leaves the contrasts unchanged when all tips are contemporaneous. Under BM with drift \(\mu\), a tip at height \(h\) has expected value \(x_{\text{root}} + \mu h\), so on an ultrametric tree of depth \(T\) every tip has the same expectation, \(x_{\text{root}} + \mu T\). The drift adds this constant to every tip and changes nothing else: the covariances are those of BM without drift. Every value used by the contrast algorithm is either a tip value or a weighted average of tip values with weights summing to one, so each has the same expectation, and every contrast, being a difference of two such values, has expectation zero and the same variance as without drift. The contrasts therefore have exactly the distribution they would have without drift. No statistic computed from the contrasts, and more generally no statistic that is invariant to a common shift of all tips, can detect a trend from contemporaneous tips. This is the formal reason why the adequacy check in Table 11.3 flags the trend scenario no more often than pure Brownian motion. With non-contemporaneous tips the cancellation fails and the drift becomes visible, which is what the drift regression of Section 11.13.4 exploits.

D.2.1 Fitch and Sankoff algorithms

In Fitch’s downpass (from tips to root), each node \(v\) with children \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) is assigned a set of candidate states: \[\begin{equation} S_v = \begin{cases} S_{c_1} \cap S_{c_2} & \text{if } S_{c_1} \cap S_{c_2} \neq \emptyset, \\ S_{c_1} \cup S_{c_2} & \text{otherwise (cost} + 1\text{)}. \end{cases} \tag{D.4} \end{equation}\] The total number of unions is the parsimony score. An uppass from the root then refines the sets into the most parsimonious reconstructions at each node.

Sankoff’s generalization allows an arbitrary cost \(c_{ij}\) for a change from state \(i\) to state \(j\). The downpass computes, for each node \(v\) and state \(i\), the minimum cost \(S_v(i)\) of the subtree below \(v\) given that \(v\) is in state \(i\): \[\begin{equation} S_v(i) = \sum_{c \in \text{children}(v)} \min_{j} \left[ c_{ij} + S_c(j) \right], \tag{D.5} \end{equation}\] with \(S_v(i) = 0\) if tip \(v\) is in state \(i\) and \(\infty\) otherwise. The minimum over \(i\) of \(S_{\text{root}}(i)\) is the parsimony score. The recursion has the structure of Equation (D.6) with minimum and addition in place of sum and product, which is why Sankoff parsimony and likelihood pruning are often presented together.

The function below implements the Fitch downpass and checks its score against phangorn.

fitch_down <- function(tree, x) {
  n <- Ntip(tree)
  sets <- vector("list", n + tree$Nnode)
  for (i in 1:n) sets[[i]] <- as.character(x[tree$tip.label[i]])
  cost <- 0
  parents <- rev(unique(rev(reorder(tree, "postorder")$edge[, 1])))
  for (v in parents) {                       # each node after all its children
    ch <- tree$edge[tree$edge[, 1] == v, 2]
    I <- Reduce(intersect, sets[ch])
    if (length(I) > 0) sets[[v]] <- I else {
      sets[[v]] <- Reduce(union, sets[ch]); cost <- cost + 1 }
  }
  list(sets = sets, cost = cost)
}

D.2.2 Pruning and conditional likelihoods by hand

Felsenstein’s pruning algorithm computes the conditional likelihood \(L_v(i)\), the probability of the tip states below node \(v\) given that \(v\) is in state \(i\), from the tips toward the root: \[\begin{equation} L_v(i) = \prod_{c \in \text{children}(v)} \sum_{j} P_{ij}(t_c)\, L_c(j), \qquad L(\theta) = \sum_i \pi_i\, L_{\text{root}}(i), \tag{D.6} \end{equation}\] with \(L_v(i) = 1\) for the observed state of a tip and 0 otherwise. The function below implements the recursion, with transition probabilities computed from an eigendecomposition of \(\mathbf{Q}\) (Section A.6).

Pt <- function(Q, t) {
  e <- eigen(Q)
  Re(e$vectors %*% diag(exp(e$values * t)) %*% solve(e$vectors))
}
prune <- function(tree, x, Q) {
  tree <- reorder(tree, "postorder")
  n <- Ntip(tree); K <- ncol(Q)
  L <- matrix(1, n + tree$Nnode, K, dimnames = list(NULL, colnames(Q)))
  L[1:n, ] <- 0
  L[cbind(1:n, match(as.character(x[tree$tip.label]), colnames(Q)))] <- 1
  for (e in seq_len(nrow(tree$edge))) {
    par <- tree$edge[e, 1]; ch <- tree$edge[e, 2]
    L[par, ] <- L[par, ] * (Pt(Q, tree$edge.length[e]) %*% L[ch, ])
  }
  L
}

D.2.3 Joint reconstruction: the Pupko algorithm

Pupko et al. (2000) find the joint reconstruction of Equation (12.2) by dynamic programming, the max-product analogue of pruning. For each non-root node \(c\) with parent \(a\), and each possible parent state \(i\), define \[\begin{equation} \Lambda_c(i) = \max_j \left[ P_{ij}(t_c) \prod_{d \in \text{children}(c)} \Lambda_d(j) \right], \qquad C_c(i) = \arg\max_j \left[ \cdots \right], \tag{D.7} \end{equation}\] where for a tip \(c\) in observed state \(s\), \(\Lambda_c(i) = P_{is}(t_c)\). At the root, choose \(\hat x_{\text{root}} = \arg\max_i \pi_i \prod_{d} \Lambda_d(i)\), and then trace back down the tree: each child takes the state \(C_c(\hat x_a)\) recorded for its parent’s chosen state. The implementation below works on the log scale and is checked against ancr(..., type = "joint").

joint_pupko <- function(tree, x, Q, pi) {
  tree <- reorder(tree, "postorder")
  n <- Ntip(tree); K <- ncol(Q); states <- colnames(Q); N <- n + tree$Nnode
  S <- matrix(0, N, K)                   # sum of log Lambda over children
  best <- matrix(NA_integer_, N, K)      # C_c(i): best child state, parent i
  for (e in seq_len(nrow(tree$edge))) {
    par <- tree$edge[e, 1]; ch <- tree$edge[e, 2]
    lP <- log(Pt(Q, tree$edge.length[e]))
    if (ch <= n) {
      j <- match(as.character(x[tree$tip.label[ch]]), states)
      lv <- lP[, j]; b <- rep(j, K)
    } else {
      M <- lP + matrix(S[ch, ], K, K, byrow = TRUE)
      lv <- apply(M, 1, max); b <- apply(M, 1, which.max)
    }
    best[ch, ] <- b; S[par, ] <- S[par, ] + lv
  }
  r <- n + 1; st <- integer(N); st[r] <- which.max(log(pi) + S[r, ])
  pre <- reorder(tree, "cladewise")
  for (e in seq_len(nrow(pre$edge)))
    st[pre$edge[e, 2]] <- best[pre$edge[e, 2], st[pre$edge[e, 1]]]
  list(nodes = setNames(states[st[r:N]], r:N), logL = max(log(pi) + S[r, ]))
}

D.2.4 Sampling a stochastic map

A stochastic character map is drawn in three steps, using the conditional likelihoods \(L_v(i)\) from a pruning pass.

  1. Root state. Draw the root state \(i\) with probability \(\pi_i L_{\text{root}}(i) / \sum_j \pi_j L_{\text{root}}(j)\).
  2. Node states, from root to tips. For each child \(c\) of a node \(a\) whose state \(i\) has been drawn, draw the state \(j\) of \(c\) with probability \[\begin{equation} P(X_c = j \mid X_a = i, D) = \frac{P_{ij}(t_c)\, L_c(j)}{\sum_{l} P_{il}(t_c)\, L_c(l)}. \tag{D.8} \end{equation}\] Together, steps 1 and 2 draw a joint assignment of node states from its exact posterior.
  3. Branch histories. For each branch, simulate the history of the character between the now-fixed start and end states, conditional on those endpoints. The simplest method is rejection sampling: simulate forward from the start state using exponential waiting times with rate \(-q_{ii}\) in state \(i\) and jump probabilities \(q_{ij}/(-q_{ii})\), and accept the history only if it ends in the required state. Rejection is inefficient on long branches and for improbable endpoints. Uniformization instead draws the number of potential changes on a branch from a Poisson distribution with a rate \(\mu \ge \max_i(-q_{ii})\), and then the states at those events from the discrete-time chain with transition matrix \(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q}/\mu\), conditional on the endpoints; it never rejects. Irvahn and Minin (2014) built on uniformization a stochastic mapping algorithm that avoids matrix exponentiation altogether, which pays off when the state space is large.

Averages over many maps estimate posterior expectations of any function of the history, such as the number of changes of each type or the time spent in each state. The analytical alternative for expected counts and dwelling times is the method of Minin and Suchard (2008).

D.2.5 Hidden-rate models

With two hidden rate categories, slow (S) and fast (F), a character with observed states \(1, \ldots, k\) has \(2k\) hidden states, and the rate matrix has block structure: \[\begin{equation} \mathbf{Q} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{Q}_S - \mathbf{D}_{SF} & \mathbf{D}_{SF} \\ \mathbf{D}_{FS} & \mathbf{Q}_F - \mathbf{D}_{FS} \end{pmatrix}, \tag{D.9} \end{equation}\] where \(\mathbf{Q}_S\) and \(\mathbf{Q}_F\) are the rate matrices for observed-state changes within each category (with their own diagonals) and \(\mathbf{D}_{SF}\), \(\mathbf{D}_{FS}\) are diagonal matrices of switching rates between categories. At a tip with observed state \(s\), the partial likelihood is 1 for both hidden states \(s_S\) and \(s_F\) and 0 otherwise, so the pruning algorithm sums over the hidden category automatically. Marginal reconstructions of the observed state are obtained by summing the reconstructed probabilities of \(s_S\) and \(s_F\), as in the corHMM example of Section 12.15.8. Two identifiability issues are worth knowing. The labels of the categories can be swapped without changing the likelihood unless the model constrains, for example, the rates in \(\mathbf{Q}_S\) to be smaller than those in \(\mathbf{Q}_F\); and with one character on a small tree the switching rates are typically poorly estimated, so that the model can fit no better than the ordinary Mk model even when rates do vary.

D.2.6 Root treatments for a rare feature, computed exactly

Section 12.4.5 argued that, for a feature attested in only a few branches, the root reconstruction depends on the root treatment as much as on the data. For a binary character the Bayesian answer can be computed exactly, by integrating the pruning likelihood over the two rates on a grid, because the transition probabilities have the closed form \(P_{01}(t) = \pi_1 (1 - e^{-(q_{01} + q_{10})t})\) and \(P_{10}(t) = \pi_0 (1 - e^{-(q_{01} + q_{10})t})\), with \(\pi_1 = q_{01} / (q_{01} + q_{10})\) the stationary frequency of state 1 (Section A.7). The feature below is present in two languages of subgroup A and two of subgroup C, on opposite sides of the root of the toy tree; both rates have Exponential(1) priors.

# root conditional likelihoods L0, L1 for vectors of rates (closed form)
root_L <- function(q01, q10) {
  tr <- reorder(tree, "postorder"); n <- Ntip(tr)
  L <- array(1, c(n + tr$Nnode, 2, length(q01)))
  for (i in 1:n) {
    L[i, , ] <- 0; L[i, rare[tr$tip.label[i]] + 1, ] <- 1 }
  a <- q01 + q10; p1 <- q01 / a
  for (e in seq_len(nrow(tr$edge))) {
    pa <- tr$edge[e, 1]; d <- tr$edge[e, 2]
    ex <- exp(-a * tr$edge.length[e])
    P01 <- p1 * (1 - ex); P10 <- (1 - p1) * (1 - ex)
    L[pa, 1, ] <- L[pa, 1, ] * ((1 - P01) * L[d, 1, ] + P01 * L[d, 2, ])
    L[pa, 2, ] <- L[pa, 2, ] * (P10 * L[d, 1, ] + (1 - P10) * L[d, 2, ])
  }
  list(L0 = L[n + 1, 1, ], L1 = L[n + 1, 2, ])
}

D.2.7 A test for historical signal in one character

Harmon (2019) proposes a blunt check of whether a character carries any information about its history. The comparison model ignores the tree and draws the state of each language independently, with probability \(p_k\) for state \(k\); its maximized log-likelihood is \(\sum_k n_k \log(n_k/n)\), where \(n_k\) languages are in state \(k\). As the rates of an Mk model grow, the tip states become independent draws from the stationary distribution, so the Mk likelihood approaches this value along a flat ridge. D.3.1 The Brownian likelihood

Under Brownian motion the tip values are multivariate normal, \(\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}_n(\mu\mathbf{1}, \sigma^2\mathbf{C})\) (Equation (13.2)), so the likelihood is the multivariate normal density (Section A.10):

\[\begin{equation} L(\mu, \sigma^2 \mid \mathbf{x}, \tau) = \frac{\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \mu\mathbf{1})^\top (\sigma^2\mathbf{C})^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \mu\mathbf{1})\right]}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\, \det(\sigma^2\mathbf{C})^{1/2}}. \tag{D.10} \end{equation}\]

Setting the derivatives of its logarithm with respect to \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) to zero gives the maximum-likelihood estimates

\[\begin{equation} \hat\mu = (\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1} \mathbf{1})^{-1}\, \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1} \mathbf{x}, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{(\mathbf{x} - \hat\mu\mathbf{1})^\top \mathbf{C}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \hat\mu\mathbf{1})}{n}. \tag{D.11} \end{equation}\]

The estimate \(\hat\mu\) is the GLS mean, a weighted average of the tip values with weights \(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{C}^{-1}\) (the same estimator as the Brownian homeland of Equation (11.6)). The REML estimate of \(\sigma^2\) uses the divisor \(n - 1\) instead of \(n\), and it equals the mean squared standardized independent contrast (Section D.3.4).

D.3.2 Covariances under the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck and early-burst models

For the OU process of Equation (13.3) on an ultrametric tree of depth \(T\), with the root value drawn from the stationary distribution, the covariance between two tips separated by a patristic distance \(d_{ij} = 2(T - s_{ij})\) is

\[\begin{equation} \operatorname{Cov}(x_i, x_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}\, e^{-\alpha d_{ij}}. \tag{D.12} \end{equation}\]

Covariance thus decays exponentially with the distance between languages, rather than linearly as under BM. As \(\alpha \to 0\) the stationary variance \(\sigma^2 / (2\alpha)\) diverges, which is why software either fixes the root value or conditions on it when \(\alpha\) is small; the two parameterizations are labelled OUfixedRoot and OUrandomRoot in phylolm.

For the early-burst model, in which the Brownian rate changes as \(\sigma^2(t) = \sigma_0^2 e^{rt}\), the covariance between two tips is the integral of the rate over their shared history:

\[\begin{equation} C_{ij} = \int_0^{s_{ij}} \sigma_0^2 e^{rt}\, dt = \sigma_0^2\, \frac{e^{r s_{ij}} - 1}{r}. \tag{D.13} \end{equation}\]

With \(r < 0\) most of the variance is generated early, so deep splits dominate the covariance structure. On an ultrametric tree, the delta transformation of Section 13.3.4 maps \(C_{ij}\) to \(C_{ij}^{\delta}\); delta and EB are therefore alternative ways of concentrating change early or late.

D.3.3 Signal statistics

Blomberg’s K. Let \(\text{MSE}_0 = (\mathbf{x} - \hat\mu\mathbf{1})^\top(\mathbf{x} - \hat\mu\mathbf{1})/(n-1)\) measure dispersion around the phylogenetic mean while ignoring the tree, and \(\text{MSE} = (\mathbf{x} - \hat\mu\mathbf{1})^\top\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \hat\mu\mathbf{1})/(n-1)\) measure dispersion using the tree. Then

\[\begin{equation} K = \frac{\text{MSE}_0 / \text{MSE}}{\dfrac{1}{n-1}\left[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}) - \dfrac{n}{\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\right]}, \tag{D.14} \end{equation}\]

where the denominator is the expected value of the ratio under BM, so that \(K = 1\) is the BM expectation (Blomberg et al. 2003).

Moran’s I. With weights \(w_{ij}\) (for example \(w_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}\) for patristic distance \(d_{ij}\)),

\[\begin{equation} I = \frac{n}{\sum_{i \ne j} w_{ij}} \cdot \frac{\sum_{i \ne j} w_{ij}(x_i - \bar x)(x_j - \bar x)}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}. \tag{D.15} \end{equation}\]

Its expected value under no autocorrelation is \(-1/(n-1)\).

The D statistic. For a binary trait, \(\Sigma d\) is the sum of differences between sister clades computed by a contrast-like algorithm. The observed value is compared with its mean under random reshuffling of the trait across tips (\(\overline{\Sigma d_r}\)) and under a Brownian threshold model (\(\overline{\Sigma d_b}\)):

\[\begin{equation} D = \frac{\Sigma d_{\text{obs}} - \overline{\Sigma d_b}}{\overline{\Sigma d_r} - \overline{\Sigma d_b}}. \tag{D.16} \end{equation}\]

D.3.4 Independent contrasts

Take two sister tips \(i\) and \(j\) with trait values \(x_i, x_j\) and branch lengths \(v_i, v_j\) to their common ancestor \(k\).

  1. Compute the standardized contrast \[\begin{equation} u_{ij} = \frac{x_i - x_j}{\sqrt{v_i + v_j}}. \tag{D.17} \end{equation}\] Under BM the raw difference has variance \(\sigma^2(v_i + v_j)\), so the standardized contrast has variance \(\sigma^2\) whatever the branch lengths.
  2. Estimate the value at node \(k\) as the weighted average \(x_k = (x_i/v_i + x_j/v_j)/(1/v_i + 1/v_j)\). The weights favor the descendant on the shorter branch.
  3. Lengthen the branch below \(k\) by \(v_i v_j/(v_i + v_j)\) to account for the error in the estimate of \(x_k\).
  4. Prune \(i\) and \(j\), treat \(k\) as a tip, and repeat.

A tree with \(n\) tips yields \(n - 1\) contrasts, independent and identically distributed \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) under BM. The regression of the contrasts of \(y\) on those of \(x\) through the origin gives exactly the PGLS slope under BM (Rohlf 2001; Blomberg et al. 2012), as the chunk below confirms for the data of Section 13.5.

set.seed(31)

D.3.5 Generalized least squares and the lambda profile

For the model of Equation (13.5), the generalized least squares estimator and its covariance are

\[\begin{equation} \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{V}^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1}\, \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{V}^{-1} \mathbf{y}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{V}^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1}, \tag{D.18} \end{equation}\]

with

\[\begin{equation} \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})^\top \mathbf{V}^{-1} (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})}{n - p}. \tag{D.19} \end{equation}\]

When \(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{I}\), these reduce to the familiar OLS formulas. GLS is OLS on transformed data. Factor \(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top\), for example by a Cholesky decomposition, and premultiply the model by \(\mathbf{L}^{-1}\):

\[\begin{equation} \mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \mathbf{L}^{-1}\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{L}^{-1}\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}. \tag{D.20} \end{equation}\]

The transformed residuals are independent with equal variance, so OLS on the transformed data is optimal (the Gauss-Markov theorem; Section A.11). The Galton simulation in Section 13.1 uses the upper-triangular factor \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{V}^{-1}\), which is an equivalent whitening.

When \(\lambda\) is estimated jointly, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) and \(\hat\sigma^2\) follow from Equations (D.18) and (D.19) for each value of \(\lambda\), with \(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{C}(\lambda)\). Substituting them into the log-likelihood gives the profile log-likelihood of \(\lambda\) (Freckleton et al. 2002):

\[\begin{equation} \ell(\lambda) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2_\lambda) - \frac{1}{2}\log\det \mathbf{C}(\lambda) - \frac{n}{2}, \tag{D.21} \end{equation}\]

with \(\hat\sigma^2_\lambda\) computed with divisor \(n\) for ML (or \(n - p\), with an adjustment, for REML). Maximizing this one-dimensional function is fast, and the profile curve gives a likelihood interval for \(\lambda\), as plotted in Figure 13.5.

D.3.6 Pagel’s independent model

With the joint states of two binary characters ordered \((0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)\), the independent model gives each character its own gain and loss rates, \(\alpha_X, \beta_X\) and \(\alpha_Y, \beta_Y\), regardless of the state of the other character:

\[\begin{equation} \mathbf{Q}_{\text{ind}} = \begin{pmatrix} \cdot & \alpha_Y & \alpha_X & 0 \\ \beta_Y & \cdot & 0 & \alpha_X \\ \beta_X & 0 & \cdot & \alpha_Y \\ 0 & \beta_X & \beta_Y & \cdot \end{pmatrix}. \tag{D.22} \end{equation}\]

It is the special case of the dependent model of Equation (13.6) with \(q_{12} = q_{34} = \alpha_Y\), \(q_{21} = q_{43} = \beta_Y\), \(q_{13} = q_{24} = \alpha_X\) and \(q_{31} = q_{42} = \beta_X\): four constraints, hence the four degrees of freedom of the likelihood-ratio test. The zeros on the anti-diagonal encode the assumption that the two characters never change at exactly the same instant. Two caveats about the \(\chi^2_4\) reference distribution are worth knowing. It is an asymptotic approximation that can be poor for small trees and for rates near zero (on the boundary of the parameter space), and the test is a test of the model, not of the number of independent co-occurring changes (Section 13.7.3). A parametric bootstrap, simulating data under the fitted independent model and refitting both models, gives a better calibrated \(p\)-value.

D.3.9 A spatiophylogenetic model by maximum likelihood

The chunk below recreates the simulated locations and trait of Section 13.10 and fits Equation (13.12) by maximum likelihood, holding the range \(\rho\) fixed, for three nested covariance structures: phylogeny with noise, space with noise, and all three components. The intercept is profiled out by GLS for each value of the variance components, which are optimized on the log scale.

set.seed(99)
lon <- fastBM(tree, sig2 = 1) + rnorm(Ntip(tree), 0, 0.3)
lat <- fastBM(tree, sig2 = 1) + rnorm(Ntip(tree), 0, 0.3)
D <- as.matrix(dist(cbind(lon, lat)))
S <- exp(-D / 1)                              # spatial correlation, range 1
Cs <- C / max(diag(C))                               # phylogenetic correlation
L_s <- t(chol(S + diag(1e-8, nrow(S))))
a_phy <- fastBM(tree, sig2 = 1 / max(diag(C)))  # phylogenetic part, variance 1
s_sp  <- drop(L_s %*% rnorm(Ntip(tree)))             # spatial part, variance 1
trait <- 0.8 * a_phy + 0.8 * s_sp + rnorm(Ntip(tree), 0, 0.5)

negloglik <- function(logv, comps, y) {
  V <- Reduce(`+`, Map(`*`, exp(logv), comps))
  R <- chol(V); Xm <- matrix(1, length(y), 1)
  Ri <- backsolve(R, diag(length(y)))                # V^-1 = Ri Ri'
  Vinv <- Ri %*% t(Ri)
  b <- solve(t(Xm) %*% Vinv %*% Xm, t(Xm) %*% Vinv %*% y)
  r <- y - Xm %*% b
  0.5 * (2 * sum(log(diag(R))) + t(r) %*% Vinv %*% r + length(y) * log(2 * pi))
}
I_n <- diag(Ntip(tree))
fits <- list(phylogeny_only = list(phylo = Cs, noise = I_n),
             space_only     = list(space = S, noise = I_n),
             both           = list(phylo = Cs, space = S, noise = I_n))
res <- t(sapply(fits, function(comps) {
  o <- optim(rep(log(0.3), length(comps)), negloglik, comps = comps,
             y = trait[tips])
  share <- setNames(rep(NA, 3), c("phylo", "space", "noise"))
  share[names(comps)] <- exp(o$par) / sum(exp(o$par))
  c(AIC = 2 * o$value + 2 * (length(comps) + 1), share)
}))

D.4.1 Borrowing as a transformation of the covariance matrix

In the horizontal transmission simulation of Section 14.14, each society \(i\) replaces a fraction \(h\) of its trait value with the value of a contact partner \(p(i)\): \(x_i' = (1 - h)\,x_i + h\,x_{p(i)}\). In matrix form, \(\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\) with \[\begin{equation} \mathbf{B} = (1 - h)\,\mathbf{I} + h\,\mathbf{P}, \tag{D.23} \end{equation}\] where \(\mathbf{P}\) has a single 1 in each row, in the column of that society’s partner. If \(\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu\mathbf{1}, \sigma^2\mathbf{C})\) under Brownian motion on the tree, then \(\mathbf{x}' \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu\mathbf{1}, \sigma^2\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^\top)\), because each row of \(\mathbf{B}\) sums to one. A PGLS that uses \(\mathbf{C}\) is therefore misspecified, and how badly depends on how different \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^\top\) is from \(\mathbf{C}\). When partners are close relatives, \(C_{i,p(i)} \approx C_{ii}\) and the transformation changes little; when partners are distant, it adds covariance where \(\mathbf{C}\) has almost none, and the regression treats similar societies as independent observations.

If the contact network and the borrowing fraction were known, a GLS with \(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^\top\) would be correctly specified. The chunk below recreates the tree and the geographic partners of the chapter, and compares the false positive rates of PGLS with \(\mathbf{C}\) and of GLS with \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^\top\) at \(h = 0.3\).

set.seed(12)
htree <- pbtree(n = 60, scale = 5)
htree$tip.label <- sprintf("S%02d", 1:60)
Ch <- vcv(htree)
geo <- as.matrix(dist(matrix(runif(120), 60, 2))); diag(geo) <- Inf
partner <- apply(geo, 1, which.min)                 # nearest neighbor in space
h <- 0.3
P <- matrix(0, 60, 60); P[cbind(1:60, partner)] <- 1
B <- (1 - h) * diag(60) + h * P
gls_p <- function(y, x, V) {
  Wv <- chol(solve(V))
  coef(summary(lm(Wv %*% y ~ Wv %*% cbind(1, x) - 1)))[2, 4]
}
V_borrow <- B %*% Ch %*% t(B)
set.seed(13)
p <- replicate(300, {
  x <- drop(B %*% fastBM(htree)); y <- drop(B %*% fastBM(htree))
  c(PGLS_tree = gls_p(y, x, Ch), GLS_borrowing = gls_p(y, x, V_borrow))
})
rowMeans(p < 0.05)                                 # false positive rates
#>     PGLS_tree GLS_borrowing 
#>        0.6067        0.0600

The GLS that knows the contact structure has close to the nominal 5% false positive rate. In practice the network and \(h\) are unknown, so they have to be modeled: neighbor-graph models (Towner et al. 2012) and spatial covariance components (Section 13.10) are approximations to \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^\top\) in which the contact structure is represented by geographic proximity and its strength is estimated from the data.
