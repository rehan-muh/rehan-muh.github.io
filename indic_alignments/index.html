indic alignments

Open in Google Sheets

Edits will be saved to Google Sheets as you make. Enter your (/a) name and the password to edit alignments.

Click a segment to change it, using the IPA shown in the table. Leave it empty to mark it lost. Press Enter for the next segment, and Esc to leave it. The green and outlined columns can be changed in the same way; empty one to take it out of the row. When no segment has the cursor, M switches Mark morphology on or off, G switches Add gap column on or off, Esc goes back to typing, and U takes back your last change. In Mark morphology, click a segment to mark or unmark it in that row, or click a letter of the ancestor, at the top of the table, to mark or unmark it in every row of the set. The heavy black line of a compound seam can go too: in Mark morphology, click the × on the line to take the seam away in every row of the set, or the | on any other line between two letters to put one there. In Add gap column, the × beside a letter of the ancestor takes that segment out of the ancestor in every row of the set; what stood under it stays as an added segment. The + before a letter (and after the last) adds a segment to the ancestor there: type it and press Enter (Esc to cancel). Every row gets a gap under it; type the reflexes in, or click an added segment beside it to move it in. The + of a green or outlined column puts an ancestor segment over that column: the segments rows have there stand under it, and the other rows get a gap. ⌫ deletes a whole column in every row of the set, the segments in it included, so those rows no longer spell their cited form; U takes it back. In typing mode, a row's empty cell in a green or outlined column takes a segment too, and so does the ancestor at the top of the table: change a letter there and it changes in every row of the set (empty it to take it out of the ancestor), or type a segment at the top of a green or outlined column to put it over that column. In any mode, drag a segment to move it: within its row onto a gap (-) under an ancestor segment, or into a green or outlined column, where it goes before the segment the row has there. Drag an ancestor letter onto a green or outlined column to move it there in every row of the set. If there is no column to drop it in, the small + at the top of the table, in typing mode, adds an empty one. Setting a pair back to its first alignment removes the edit. To move a segment along its row, switch on Add gap column: each click on ← or → moves it one column. From under an ancestor segment, one step puts it in the gap column beside it, and a second puts it under the next ancestor segment, if that one has nothing under it. Click a segment to pick it, and the arrow keys move it the same way. A segment never jumps over another of its row; move that one first, or add a column with +. A row marked uncertain leaves its table, and a set you tick as checked or as a problem set leaves the page. Choose uncertain rows, sets checked, or problem sets under Status to see them, and untick one to put it back. Rows whose alignment was automatically repaired are under to look at again, and the sheet menu says which sheets hold them. Their sets come first on the page and stay there even when ticked as checked. Once you have looked at such a row, press looked at again beside it, or tick looked at again at the top of its set for all of them: the row is saved as it stands, under your name, and leaves to look at again. Saving the row, or clearing its note, does the same.

A geminate is one segment with the length mark: kː, kʰː, in the ancestor and the reflexes alike (Pāli bhatta is bʰ a tː a). Typing kk or kkʰ in a cell writes kː or kʰː. A cluster that came out as a geminate has it under its first member (k t over tː -), unless a nasal, liquid or glide opens the cluster (r p over - pː). Only a geminate split by a compound seam (ud+dāla), or with one half marked as morphology, keeps two columns.

Sources. Turner's Comparative Dictionary as digitized in JAMBU (Arora et al. 2023); Pischel, Grammatik der Prakrit-Sprachen (1900), in Subhadra Jhā's translation; Oberlies, Pāli (2001); IE-CoR (Heggarty et al. 2023). Turner's comparanda from outside Indo-Aryan are left out.

Where and when. Each row says where and when its form is attested, as far as the sources tell; hover for the basis. An inscription gives its site and the date of its edict (Kalsi, 260–250 BCE). A form Pischel cites from a text gets the earliest of the texts he cites, with its date and, where it has one, its place: the Jain canon was written down at Valabhī, Hemacandra worked at Aṇahilapāṭaka. A modern form gets JAMBU's point for its dialect or language, and the date of Turner's source for the language where his bibliography names one (Platts's dictionary of 1884 for Hindi-Urdu), else the span of his sources, up to 1966. The heading of a set gives the earliest text Turner names for an Old Indo-Aryan headword (RV., MBh.). Literary Middle Indo-Aryan has a period and no place. The dates of texts are conventional ranges.

Language names. A language has one name on every sheet, the name JAMBU gives it, with a variety after a colon (Prakrit: Ardhamagadhi, Ashokan Prakrit: Kalsi). Pischel's abbreviations are written out: AMg. is Prakrit: Ardhamagadhi, M. Prakrit: Maharashtri, Ś. Prakrit: Shauraseni, A. Apabhramsha. Oberlies's Aśokan citations go under the site of the inscription, with the edict in the row's details. IE-CoR's Eastern Pahari is Nepali, and its Hindi and Urdu are Hindi-Urdu: Hindi and Hindi-Urdu: Urdu. Where the name says less than the book, the book's label is in the details: Kī. for Kramadīśvara, PG. and VG. for the two Pallava grants. A set with several forms from one language has a row for each, all under the same name.

Morphology. An ancestor's ending is marked only where the words of a set differ over it, so an ending every reflex continues carries no mark. Each mark gives its rule. 3sg verb ending, 3sg -ai, final anusvāra, and infinitive are the rules used before a fit. Suffix marks come from Turner's own word families: forms of one language under one headword that share a stem show what the suffixes are, and a suffix is cut only if the stem then fits the ancestor better. Each stem is then aligned alone, and so is each member of a compound. Ending -am and the like are a table of Sanskrit endings, applied to words cited without a hyphen, and they overmark: a short noun can pass as inflected. Marked in review came from the reviewers.

Learned forms. A Prakrit ancestor is tagged probable learned form if it keeps the Sanskrit stop between vowels, which Prakrit normally loses. A stop at the start of a compound member or after a prefix counts as word-initial and is not tagged, and neither is a dialect's regular change (Śaurasenī -d- from -t-). This is a rule of thumb: a tagged form may still be a plain inherited word in some dialect.

Repeats. A row with the same ancestor, language, and reflex is shown once, and it says where else it occurs. The same reflex under a different ancestor is a separate comparison and stays.

Checks. Every row is checked against its cited form: the reflex cells, green and outlined ones included, must spell it. A row that does not is flagged alignment does not spell the cited form, and a reviewed row whose segments are all there but out of order (a metathesis) is flagged segments aligned in another order than the word.

Status. Reviewed pairs show the reviewed alignment. Excluded in review pairs were dropped from the fits. Uncertain rows are set aside from their tables and listed under Status. Everything else is the machine alignment.
